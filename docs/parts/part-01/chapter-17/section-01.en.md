# P1-17.1 What Evidence Should Forecasts Rely On?

> Section ID: `P1-17.1`
> Version: `v2026.07.20`

Part 1 Chapter 16 covered how AI can be applied to personal learning, work automation, and small projects. Chapter 17 turns to `the future of AI`.

But `forecasting` requires more caution than ordinary concept explanation. A plausible future story generated by AI can sound persuasive, but without evidence it is creative speculation rather than learning material.

This boundary connects directly to the `accountability` discussed in Chapter 15. Readers should keep asking:

> who said it?  
> what evidence was used?  
> what part is interpretation, and what part is being stated like fact?

This section explains what kinds of evidence should be required when writing about AI forecasts. How to read actual articles and reports is covered in P1-17.2. How to separate predictions from working hypotheses is covered in P1-17.3.

| Topic | Question in this section |
| --- | --- |
| forecast | who said what about the future, and on what basis? |
| scenario | is it a division of possible cases, or a firm prediction? |
| indicator | is there measurable evidence such as investment, jobs, performance, or policy? |
| stake | what interests does the institution or company making the claim have? |

## Grounding Forecasts in Evidence

- Explain why forecast sentences are risky when written without sources.
- Distinguish the different weights carried by different kinds of forecast material.
- Explain why indicators should be read as directional signals rather than as fixed conclusions about the future.

## Three Standards

| Standard | Why it matters | Level of understanding needed here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| forecast sentences should not be written without sources | This prevents plausible future claims generated by AI from being copied as if they were facts. | It is enough to understand that the speaker, date, and evidence should be written together. |
| different forecast materials carry different weights | This prevents news, reports, and company announcements from being read as if they had the same role. | It is enough to understand that policy documents, research reports, and columns serve different purposes. |
| indicators are directional signals, not conclusions by themselves | This reduces overgeneralization from one number or trend. | It is enough to understand that more investment or better benchmark performance is only a signal, not a guaranteed future. |

## Do Not Write Forecast Sentences Without Sources

In a learning document like this, a forecast sentence should include at least the following:

> who said it  
> when they said it  
> in what material or context they said it  
> whether the statement is a prediction, scenario, or policy proposal

For example, the book should not write:

> AI will replace all jobs.

Instead, it should write something closer to:

> the World Economic Forum's 2025 `Future of Jobs Report` analyzed technological change, economic uncertainty, and demographic change as major drivers that may reshape employment and skills between 2025 and 2030

That sentence is not prophecy. It conveys the scope and claim of a report.

## Distinguish Types of Evidence

Not all forecast material has the same weight.

| Type of material | Strength | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| policy document | useful for checking institutional direction and standards | does not guarantee actual market outcomes |
| research report | usually provides clearer indicators and methodology | assumptions and sampling still need to be checked |
| company announcement | reveals product direction and investment intention | may mix evidence with promotion and self-interest |
| news report | shows events and disputes quickly | risky if generalized from one article alone |
| column | useful for seeing interpretation and framing | fact and opinion still have to be separated |

That is why this chapter does not present forecasts as settled truth. It first states the nature of the source.

## Indicators Show Direction, Not Final Conclusions

Materials such as Stanford HAI's `AI Index Report` are useful because they track long-term indicators such as research activity, performance, investment, policy, and industry adoption.

But an `indicator` is not the same thing as a conclusion.

> investment increased  
> -> that may signal concentrated attention and capital  
> -> but it does not mean every service will succeed
>
> benchmark performance improved  
> -> that means the system improved on a particular evaluation  
> -> but it does not mean the system is always reliable in real work

When writing forecasts, indicators and interpretation should stay separate.

## Checklist

- You can attach who said a forecast, when, and in what context.
- You can distinguish prediction, scenario, policy proposal, and company announcement.
- You can separate indicators from interpretation.
- You can distinguish fact and opinion when using news or columns as evidence.
- You can handle forecast sentences by separating `source`, `type of material`, and `indicator versus interpretation`.
